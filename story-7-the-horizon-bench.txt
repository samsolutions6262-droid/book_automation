The Bench With a Good View of Nothing
=====================================

At the end of the breakwater, past the sail shop and the parking
lot everyone fought over, there was a bench that faced open water
and open nothing. No lighthouse. No island. Just horizon.

It had been installed by the town council by mistake — ordered in
a batch meant for the harbor overlook, delivered at the wrong pier —
and it had never been moved, because moving things required
budgets, and not moving things required nothing at all.

People found it anyway.

First the insomniacs, who liked a place where night was allowed
to be as big as it felt. Then a retired ferry captain who came
every Tuesday to look straight at nothing and call it "keeping
current." Then teenagers, who carved nothing rude into it, only
initials and the year, like it was less a bench than a ledger.

Dr. Achebe found it the week her hospital closed. She sat for an
hour and realized no one had asked her opinion the entire time,
and that this was, unexpectedly, the most restful thing that had
happened to her in years.

By the following summer there was a waiting rhythm: dawn for the
grievers, midmorning for the retired, dusk for anyone whose plans
had recently failed. Nobody organized it. Nobody wrote it down.
It was simply understood, the way a threshold is understood.

The council eventually sent a surveyor to assess moving the bench
to the harbor overlook, where it could have a proper view.

The surveyor's report was one sentence long:

"Recommend against. View already in use."

                            THE END
