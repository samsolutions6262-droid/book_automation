The Map of Everyone Who Ever Got Lost Here
==========================================

In the visitor center of the Marrow Creek Preserve hung a map so
dense with pins that the forest beneath it was nearly invisible:
every person officially reported lost in the preserve since 1952,
one pin each, stuck at the point where searchers last picked up
their trail.

The ranger, Corazón, added pins with a ceremony. Story first, pin
second. The lost got their sentences read aloud — the boy chasing
a heron, the bride whose whole party went after her, the man who
had walked in to be alone and was granted eleven days of it
before anyone worried.

Nobody ever died on the record. Not because they didn't, but
because the map's founder, Ranger Aldous, had made a rule in
1953: the map is for finding, not for counting.

So it stayed a map of almost, and of nearly, and of found by
afternoon. And over the decades the preserve's visitors learned
to read it like weather. Where the pins clustered, families
taught their kids landmarks. Where the pins were sparse, the
trailside benches got repaired.

Corazón retired the same year the hundredth pin went in. On her
last day, she took down the map, healed the old holes in the
paper with archival tape, and hung a blank map beside it.

"For the next seventy years," she told the new ranger, "try to
keep it that boring."

It is, at this writing, still mostly boring. There are four pins.
One is from a drone. One was reported lost on purpose, by a poet
who wanted, just once, to be a story told in rhyme — he came out
on his own, grinning, two days later.

                            THE END
