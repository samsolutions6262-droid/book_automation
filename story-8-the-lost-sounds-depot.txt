The Night Shift at the Lost Sounds Depot
========================================

Most cities have a lost-and-found. Halvorsen ran the one for
sounds.

A man came in twice a year to collect his dog's bark, which
insisted on slipping off the dog whenever they crossed a certain
bridge. A singer left her laugh behind at a wedding in 2011 and
hadn't laughed since, properly. Balloons were filed under "P,"
crowds under "C," every "I love you" spoken too quietly to be
heard — those had their own wing.

Halvorsen's shift ran from midnight to six, because that was when
the sounds got homesick. At 2 a.m. the shelves would rustle: a
doorbell from a demolished house ringing without being pressed,
the squeak of a shoe whose owner had died, and once — memorably —
an entire rainstorm from 1974, pacing its container like a tiger.

"You have to let them visit," his predecessor had told him.
"Filed is not the same as kept."

So every night, from 2 to 3, Halvorsen opened the wing and let
the sounds out into the depot, where they echoed off the rafters,
greeted each other in unintelligible ways, and made a mess of
the labels. At 3 they herded themselves back, generally, except for
one — a train whistle that always required a firm hand and a
promise of tomorrow.

On his last shift before retirement, Halvorsen opened every
container at once, just once, for a full hour. The depot roared
and sang and rained. And when it was time to close, he stepped
outside and heard, in the ordinary street silence, every sound
he had ever cared for riding behind him like a slow parade.

                            THE END
